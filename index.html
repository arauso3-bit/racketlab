      Spec guide

      Start here. These are the numbers you’ll see on a racquet or a string package, and what each one does to the way it plays. Once you think you understand the specs, move to the Rackets tab to see how popular rackets stack up. If you know what specs you want, go to the Racket Builder.

      Based mainly on Tennis Warehouse’s How to Pick the Right Tennis Racquet, with definitions from their Guide to Racquet and String Terms. Entries marked general knowledge cover things those guides don’t, so treat them as common wisdom rather than Tennis Warehouse’s word.

      Racquet specs

      The dark label is what happens when the number goes up, and the light label is what happens when it goes down. ✓ a benefit − a cost.

      Head size

      The area of the hitting surface, in square inches.

      Bigger head

      • More power
      • Bigger sweet spot
      • More forgiving on off-center hits

      Smaller head

      • More control and precision
      • Tends to suit stronger players
      • Less forgiving when you miss the center
      Midsize85–95Small midplus96–99Large midplus100–104Oversize105+

      Most racquets today are 97 to 100 sq in, a blend of power and control that works for many players. Tennis Warehouse’s own classes are midsize 85–96, midplus 96–106, oversize 107–115 and super oversize 116+. The tool splits that range more finely.

      Length

      Standard is 27 in. Frames run from 26.5 to 29 in, and 29 is the tournament limit.

      Longer frame

      • More reach on groundstrokes
      • More leverage on serves
      • A little more power
      • Higher swingweight, so harder to swing

      Standard length

      • Easier to maneuver
      • Less reach and leverage
      Standard27 inTournament limit29 in

      Many long frames are light so they stay maneuverable. This tool doesn’t model length.

      Weight

      How much the frame weighs. Strung weight, with strings added, is what you actually swing.

      Heavier

      • More power
      • More stable on contact
      • Less shock to the arm
      • Better “plow-through”
      • Harder to swing

      Lighter

      • Easier to swing and position
      • Easier to generate spin
      • Quicker hands at the net
      Lightunder 270 gMedium270–312 gHeavy312–340 gVery heavy340+ g

      You can add weight to a frame but can’t take it away, so if you’re unsure, start lighter.

      Balance

      Where the frame’s weight sits, measured from the butt of the handle.

      Head-heavy

      • More power and plow-through for the same weight
      • Common in very light frames

      Head-light

      • Feels lighter and swings faster
      • Common in heavy frames
      Even343 mm (13.5 in)One point1/8 in (3.2 mm)Extra head-light8+ points

      A 27 in frame that balances at 12.5 in is 8 points head-light.

      Swingweight

      How heavy the frame feels while you swing it. It depends on weight, length, balance and head size.

      Higher

      • More power
      • More stability
      • More comfort
      • Harder to swing

      Lower

      • Easier to accelerate
      • Easier to position
      • Less stable against heavy pace
      Low305 and belowMedium310–325Highabove 325

      Advanced players tend to like higher numbers, beginners and intermediates lower. This tool estimates swingweight from weight, balance and head size.

      Stiffness (RA)

      A flex score for the frame. Lower numbers bend more.

      Stiffer

      • More power: the frame bends less, so it takes less energy from the ball
      • More shock to wrist, elbow and shoulder

      More flexible

      • More feel
      • Gentler on the arm
      • Less power
      Flexible63 and belowMedium64–67Stiff68 and up

      Myth: flexible frames “catapult” the ball. The ball is gone in 3 to 5 milliseconds, before the frame can spring back, so frames only absorb energy. With arm or shoulder trouble, Tennis Warehouse suggests RA 66 or lower.

      Beam width

      The thickness of the frame, in millimeters.

      Wider beam

      • Stiffer frame
      • More power, through that stiffness

      Thinner beam

      • More flexible frame
      • More feel and control

      Tennis Warehouse says little beyond “wider is stiffer,” so the tool treats beam width as a small nudge on top of RA.

      String pattern

      Mains by crosses: how many vertical and horizontal strings the frame has.

      Open (fewer strings)

      • More power and a higher launch
      • More spin, as the strings snap back
      • Strings wear and break sooner

      Dense (more strings)

      • More control
      • Longer string life
      • Room for softer, thinner strings
      Very open16×18Open16×19Medium16×20Dense18×20

      Dense patterns give up some string movement, but they let you swing fast and make the spin that way.

      Forgiveness and sweet spot

      The sweet spot is the area with the most power and accuracy and the least shock.

      Bigger, wider head

      • Bigger sweet spot
      • Resists twisting on off-center hits

      Smaller, narrower head

      • Smaller sweet spot
      • Needs cleaner contact

      The yellow oval on the racket drawing is the tool’s rough picture of this. It grows with head size, swingweight and beam width.

      String specs

      Material sets the character. Gauge and tension tune it. ✓ a benefit − a cost.

      String material general knowledge

      What the string is made of sets its character.

      • Natural gutSoft, lively and crisp, with great touch. Holds tension longest.Pick it if you want the best feel and comfort and will pay for it. Great as a cross string.
      • MultifilamentSoft and cushioned with easy power. Gentle on the arm.Pick it if you want comfort on a budget or have a tender arm. It wears out faster than polyester.
      • Synthetic gutBasic and balanced: a bit of everything, nothing extreme.Pick it if you want a cheap, forgiving all-rounder for casual play.
      • Soft co-polyesterPoly control and spin with more give. Lower power, easier on the arm than firm poly.Pick it if you swing fast and want control without the harshness of firm poly.
      • Firm polyesterStiff, slippery and low power. Keeps the ball down and lasts long, but goes dead fast.Pick it if you swing hard, break strings, and want to keep the ball in. Restring often.

      Tennis Warehouse adds a note on pairing: a stiff, powerful frame with a stiff, spin-friendly string is how much of the elite modern power game is played. For recreational players, the same pairing can cause comfort problems.

      String shape partly general knowledge

      The cross-section of the string. Edges grip the ball, so shape changes bite, spin and feel.

      More edges

      • More bite and spin
      • Often firmer, with lower power
      • Can feel less predictable

      Rounder

      • More predictable response
      • Smoother, often more comfortable
      • Less bite on the ball
      • RoundSmooth and predictable. The ball leaves on a consistent line.Pick it if you want comfort and consistency, or a simple baseline.
      • TexturedRound string with a gritty surface that grips the ball a little more.Pick it if you want a bit more spin without the edgy feel of a shaped string.
      • Multi-sidedFive to eight flat faces. The corners catch the ball for more spin and control.Pick it if you want more spin than round but less harshness than square.
      • SquareFour sharp edges that dig into the ball. Most spin, a flat low-power response and a firm feel.Pick it if you swing fast and long and want heavy topspin that still lands in.
      • TwistedEdges spiral along the string. Bites like a shaped string but plays livelier.Pick it if you want edge-driven spin with more comfort and pop than a square string.

      Tennis Warehouse’s spin picks include square, pentagon, octagon and twisted-gear strings, and it describes a firm square string as its top spin performer, with a low-powered response. Shapes are mostly used on polyester, so the tool applies them only to polyester strings.

      Gauge

      String thickness. A higher number means a thinner string, and L means a half step thinner.

      Thinner

      • More feel
      • More power
      • More spin

      Thicker

      • More durable
      • Longer string life
      151.40 mm15L1.35 mm161.30 mm16L1.25 mm171.20 mm181.15 mm

      Most strings are 15 to 17. Brands measure differently: one 16 gauge may be 1.28 mm and another 1.32 mm.

      Tension partly general knowledge

      How tightly the strings are pulled, in pounds.

      Higher tension

      • More control
      • Less power
      • More shock to the arm

      Lower tension

      • More power
      • Softer feel
      • Less shock to the arm
      Machine setting60 lbActual in frameabout 50–55 lb

      The machine setting is always higher than the tension that ends up in the frame. Continuous-pull machines string about 5 to 10 percent tighter than lockout machines, so compare numbers only from the same kind of machine. A small, stiff, light frame strung tight is the harshest combination for the arm.

      Hybrid stringing partly general knowledge

      Different strings for the mains and the crosses. They can differ in type or gauge.

      Mains (vertical)

      • Do most of the spin and control work
      • Often the firmer, more durable string

      Crosses (horizontal)

      • Add feel, comfort and power
      • Often the softer string

      A common recipe is polyester mains with a soft cross like gut or multifilament. Tennis Warehouse mentions Kevlar mains with nylon crosses, but only as a last resort for people who break strings constantly. It suggests thicker nylon or polyester first, because Kevlar is extremely stiff.

      String life and tension loss partly general knowledge

      Strings lose tension and elasticity over time, until they “play dead.”

      Lasts longer

      • Thicker gauge
      • Dense pattern
      • Natural gut holds tension best

      Wears out sooner

      • Thin gauge
      • Open pattern
      • Polyester loses tension quickly

      Elasticity is how readily strings return to position after contact, and resilience is how fast they do it. As both fade, the stringbed feels dead and you work harder.
